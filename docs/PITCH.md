# FlightRisk NYC — demo script and Q&A prep

Track: **Datathon**. Everything quoted here is regenerated by `make reproduce`
and re-checked by `make verify`, which re-reads every headline number from the
metrics files and fails if the prose has drifted.

## Before the judges arrive

```bash
make data && make train        # if the artefacts are not already on disk
make app                       # http://localhost:8501
```

Open two windows: the app on tab **1 · One flight**, already set to
**22 December 2013**, and `reports/report.pdf`.

One-line answer to "what is it", if that is all there is time for:

> It tells an airport operations desk which of tomorrow's departures are going
> to land late, early enough to do something about it — and we measured what
> that is worth instead of asserting it.

## Three minutes

**0:00 — The problem, and the thing everyone gets wrong (40 s)**

> US flight delay costs about $33 billion a year. Two thirds of that lands on
> passengers, not airlines. A New York ops desk sees several hundred departures
> a day and can act on a few dozen — swap an aircraft, hold a connection. The
> constraint is attention, not information.
>
> Nearly every published model on this dataset reports AUC above 0.90. They get
> there by using `dep_delay` — how late the plane actually left. By the time
> you know that, the decision is gone. We measured the shortcut instead of
> taking it: same model, same split, **0.846 PR-AUC with it, 0.507 without**.
> Two thirds of the field's apparent skill is the observation that the plane
> left late.

**0:40 — What we built (30 s)**

> LightGBM, 67 features known before push-back, a strictly temporal split —
> train on January to August, hold out November and December. PR-AUC 0.507
> against a 0.250 base rate; the riskiest 10% of a day is 47% late.

**1:10 — Demo: one flight (35 s)** — *tab 1, pick a December flight*

> Real flight, held-out period. 61% risk. The reasons are the actual model —
> SHAP, exact, not a narrative we wrote. Here it is the gust history and no
> slack in the aircraft's rotation. Ground truth underneath: it landed 78
> minutes late. The model never saw that.

**1:45 — Demo: the day, and the honest number (40 s)** — *tab 2, then tab 3*

> This is the desk view. 22 December, 900 departures, budget of 10%. Notice
> we spend the budget *per day* — a desk can't save November's alerts for
> Christmas. That makes the number worse and the exercise real.
>
> And here is what it's worth. A 10% budget reaches **24% of all delay
> minutes**. Random alerting reaches 10%; a spreadsheet of historical route
> rates reaches 15%. So the model is 2.4× random, 1.65× the no-ML alternative.
> At a pessimistic 10% recovery rate that's $3M over two months, and $1.7M of
> it is the model rather than the budget.

**2:25 — The finding we're proudest of (25 s)** — *tab 4*

> Then we asked who the alerts reach. Coverage ranges from 38% down to 2%
> across carriers. Southwest runs late more often than JetBlue and gets a
> fortieth of the alerts, because we under-predict it by 17 points. The AUC
> can't see that. Spending the same budget proportionally closes the gap for
> 11.6% of the delay caught — and for destination size it's actually free.

**2:50 — Close (10 s)**

> Every number in the report is re-read from a metrics file by `make verify`,
> and the whole pipeline rebuilds bit-identically from the raw CSVs.

## Questions we expect

**"How do you know it isn't leaking the answer?"**
Three layers. `tests/test_features.py` enforces the feature boundary, including
that the inbound-leg feature never reads a landing that happened after our
prediction time and that the tail-number encoding is genuinely out-of-fold.
`make verify` scrambles every post-departure column and confirms the shipped
model's predictions are *exactly* unchanged. And the split is temporal, so a
leak would have to travel backwards in time.

**"0.507 doesn't sound very good."**
Against a 0.250 base rate it is double. And the honest comparison isn't to
1.0, it's to what the desk does today — which is either nothing or a
spreadsheet of route averages. We measured both: 10.0% and 14.6% of delay
minutes reached, against our 24.2%. The ceiling here is low for a real reason:
most marginal lateness is noise. Which is why we went after the tail instead —
cancellation is at 0.936.

**"Where does the $3M come from? Isn't it made up?"**
Three links in the chain and we only own the first. Ranking quality is
measured on held-out data. Unit costs are cited — $98.41 per block minute from
the carriers' own DOT Form 41 filings, $47 an hour from FAA guidance. The third
link, how much of a warned delay a desk actually recovers, nobody publishes and
we cannot measure it from a historical dataset, so we never assume it: 10% for
the headline, 2–40% swept, break-even reported. It's a ceiling on available
value, not a measured saving, and the report says so in those words.

**"Why 10% mitigation effectiveness?"**
Because it's pessimistic and it doesn't matter. Break-even is 0.11%. The
programme isn't gated on cost; it's gated on whether advance warning changes
anything at all, and answering that needs a randomised rollout, not a bigger
model.

**"Why is your precision 47% here and 64% in the README?"**
Different rules, both reported. 64% is the global top decile pooled over the
whole test period, which lets the budget be spent entirely on storm days. 47%
is per-day, which is what a desk actually faces. The impact section uses the
harder one throughout.

**"Is the fairness section just box-ticking?"**
It changed the recommendation. Ranking globally under-serves carriers the model
reads badly — Southwest at 13.5% coverage on a 33% late rate. So we allocate
the budget per carrier instead, and we priced what that costs: 11.6% of the
delay minutes caught. For destination-size quartiles the same change *gains*
1.3%, which told us the global threshold was mis-allocating in the first place.
That's a diagnostic, not a virtue signal.

**"What would you do next?"**
Not a bigger model. Swap the persistence weather for a real forecast feed —
we showed a three-hour horizon costs 0.020 PR-AUC using the crudest possible
forecast, so today's numbers are a floor. Then a randomised rollout to measure
the one thing we had to assume.

**"What's the weakest part?"**
Two things. Diversion, at 0.608 — decided in the air by weather at the
destination, which this dataset doesn't have; we report it rather than hiding
it. And the mitigation-effectiveness assumption, which is the only number in
the impact section we couldn't source.

**"Did you use AI to write this?"**
We used it the way you'd use a pair programmer — and every number it or we
produced has to survive `make verify`, which re-reads each claim from the
artefact that generated it. That check exists precisely so nothing in the prose
can be taken on trust.
